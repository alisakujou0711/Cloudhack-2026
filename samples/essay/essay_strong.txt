Essay Question 1: Why do you want to study Computer Science at NUS?
--------------------------------------------------
The first time I felt the specific thrill of computer science was at 2am, staring at a
segmentation fault in a pathfinding algorithm I had written for a school robotics competition.
I had modeled our warehouse-navigation robot's map as a grid, but a single off-by-one error in
my boundary check meant the robot occasionally tried to plan a path through a wall. Fixing that
one line taught me more about how computers actually "think" than a semester of lectures could
have. Since then I have built a Discord bot that automated attendance-tracking for my CCA of 80
members, cutting our weekly admin time from 45 minutes to under 5, and a small Android app that
helps my grandmother's neighborhood watch group log incidents in her first language. Both
projects left me wanting to understand the systems layer underneath the tools I was using —
how a database keeps data consistent under concurrent writes, how a compiler decides what code
is even valid. NUS's School of Computing draws me specifically because of the depth of its
systems and AI tracks and its proximity to Singapore's tech industry, which means the research
happening in its labs is often only a short bus ride from the companies applying it. I want to
spend the next four years moving from someone who patches bugs at 2am to someone who designs
the systems so those bugs are easier to catch in the first place.

Essay Question 2: Describe a challenge you have overcome.
--------------------------------------------------
Halfway through building the attendance bot I mentioned above, our CCA's exco unanimously voted
to scrap the project. They were worried that an unfamiliar tool would confuse the 60% of members
who barely used Discord beyond reading announcements, and a failed rollout right before our
annual showcase would embarrass the club. Rather than argue in the meeting, I asked for two
weeks to run a small pilot with just my own section of 15 members, and I promised to revert to
the old paper sign-in sheet immediately if it caused problems. During the pilot I sat with the
three members who were least comfortable with the tool and rebuilt onboarding around their
confusion points — mainly, they didn't know they needed to react to a message rather than type
a command. I rewrote the bot's welcome message around that single insight. When I brought the
pilot's 100% adoption rate and zero complaints back to the exco, they approved a full rollout.
The experience reshaped how I handle disagreement: instead of trying to win an argument with a
better pitch, I try to shrink the risk of being wrong down to something small enough that people
are willing to let me test it.
